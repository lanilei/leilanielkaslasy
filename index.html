About

Hi I'm Leilani! Welcome to my portfolio! I am an engineer who loves to combine deep technical expertise with design thinking and creative problem solving. My greatest strength is my ability to adapt and learn quickly, diving in head first to build upon my experience and rise to the task at hand. Watson Fellowship · Kabarnet School for the DeafBlind · Kabarnet, Kenya

Back to School Looks a Little Different This Year

This fall I began a residency at Kabarnet School for the DeafBlind, where I'm starting a workshop to build assistive devices for learners with disabilities, and running a curriculum with the teachers so they can keep adapting to their learners' challenges through design after I leave.

Arriving

I arrived in mid-August, a week before the school term began. The learners here are children with complex disabilities: some have combined deafblindness, some use wheelchairs, and many live with more than one.

What I Noticed

The learners' challenges were plentiful, and most came back to resources. The school operates at the edge of making ends meet; there have even been terms when teachers volunteered without pay. Much of what's considered basic support for learners with disabilities simply wasn't there.

  • Wheelchairs were poorly fitted, with no safe way to secure learners in them.
  • Communication was a huge challenge. Most learners don't communicate verbally, and while some use sign language, most don't.
  • Medical and developmental needs, like stiffness in joints and limbs, were beyond my scope as an engineer, so I knew right away I wanted to work closely with the county's occupational therapists to see what was possible.

Getting Approval

After I arrived, it turned out the project needed buy-in from Baringo County's Board of Education and Teachers Service Commission, since it was an activity involving their students and teachers. Working with local authorities to earn that approval became the first step of the project.

Setting Up the Workshop

In Nairobi I shopped for the basic power tools I expected to need, and when I arrived the school gave me an empty room. I built a cabinet, ordered tables, and painted the room to turn it into a workshop.

The Curriculum, Week by Week

After I met with the school's board of directors and principal, they were excited about the project and asked the teachers to commit three afternoons a week to the workshop. I wanted to get them making things for their learners right away, so I started with clear, step-by-step plans anyone could follow, whatever their experience with fabrication, and scaled up our activities and materials week by week.

  1. Week 1

    Setting up

    Set up the workshop and got to know the school.

  2. Week 2

    Communication cards

    Our first build: stations that every teacher rotated through, each with printed instructions.

  3. Week 3

    Built-up spoon handles

    Many learners struggled with independent activities like eating and brushing their teeth because of fine motor challenges, so we made large handles for spoons.

  4. Week 4

    Sensory curtains

    PVC-pipe sensory curtains that encourage learners to reach and stretch.

  5. Week 5

    Wheelchair support straps + independent projects

    Learners had been tied into their wheelchairs with bed sheets, so we made proper support straps. We also kicked off independent projects.

  6. Week 6

    Harnesses and cushions

    Finished the harnesses and made improved wheelchair cushions, including pommels (abductors), for better fit.

  7. Week 7

    Alternative seating

    Built chairs that give learners who use wheelchairs somewhere else to sit.

The structured weeks were productive, but I wanted to move teachers quickly from following instructions to solving problems on their own. I interviewed 40 teachers, met with 70 learners, and scoped an individualized project for each teacher and learner. With teachers' limited time, I knew much of the work would fall to me, so I made sure to bring them into the workshop at the critical points: ideating, designing, and making the first prototype together.

Braces with the Occupational Therapists

Alongside the teachers, my own project with the county's occupational therapists has been making affordable PVC-pipe braces, backslabs and elbow braces, for learners whose muscle contractures make it hard to extend their knees and elbows while they sleep. I was lucky to meet an orthopedic technologist who agreed to train me in making them, and they're nearly done. Next, I want to bring in the learners' caregivers.

E185B · Harvey Mudd College × Audio-Technica · Spring 2025 · with Max Plush, Juni Cleary & Gaven Jesse

Folding the Future of Packaging

Audio-Technica ships its turntables in expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam: cheap and protective, but not recyclable or compostable, and it breaks down into microplastics. Our brief was to replace it with a paper-based, affordable alternative that could survive the company's drop-test standard and be mass produced.

The Challenge

  • Paper only: fully paper-based materials, including cardboard, that are recyclable and/or biodegradable.
  • Protective: meet Audio-Technica's standard of a 20 lb product surviving six drops from 70 cm.
  • Practical: cost-competitive with EPS and mass producible.

Design Process

We explored corrugation manipulation, kirigami, molded cardboard pulp, and woven cardboard. Molded pulp came first: we 3D-printed a mold and tuned the recipe, finding that cornstarch worked as a binder where tapioca didn't, and that a finer, longer-blended pulp was stronger. When client feedback made clear pulp wasn't practical to manufacture at scale, we pivoted to engineering standard cardboard. The final system combined four elements:

  • Kirigami-inspired corners that fold to fit a range of box sizes, with slits so they can be reused as record display stands.
  • Firm cross-sections of stacked, glued corrugated cardboard along the sides for lateral protection.
  • Woven cardboard mesh above and below the turntable to spread and absorb impact.
  • Gap-filling barriers to stop the turntable from shifting in the box.

We also swapped the plastic dust cover wrap for a Japanese tenugui towel, inspired by furoshiki wrapping, to celebrate Audio-Technica's heritage and give customers something worth keeping.

Testing & What We Learned

Using conservation of energy, we matched the 20 lb / 70 cm standard by dropping our 5.73 lb AT-LP60X turntable from 8 ft. One round at 70 cm only chipped a plastic hinge; after two rounds at 8 ft, the damage was severe enough that we stopped short of the full protocol.

  • The sides protected by woven mesh took little to no damage, making it the most promising material.
  • The sides relying on the firm cross-sections and corners cracked, especially near the back hinges, and the turntable had too much room to move.
  • Next iteration: wrap the whole turntable in woven mesh, tighten the fit, and reinforce the hinge side.

The biggest lesson was about sustainability itself: packaging is most sustainable when people want to keep it instead of throwing it away.

Tribology Lab

Tribology is the study of friction and wear between surfaces in motion. Our lab studied how abrasive stereolithography (SLA) 3D-printed parts are, and how to tune that abrasiveness through the resin itself and through metal and ceramic additives. The Island School × Deep Creek Primary School · Eleuthera, Bahamas

Reading Outreach: One Tutoring Session at a Time

Deep Creek Primary School asked The Island School for help supporting its young readers. Our team of eight paired each Island School student with a struggling reader for weekly one-on-one tutoring, then tracked their progress to answer one question: is our help helpful?

Results

+1.7reading levels on average, pre- to post-test
+16%reading independence
+12%motivation to read
+10%focus during reading
  • Tutors spent 22 total hours with their buddies, about 3.5 hours per student, and the relationships they built were key to motivating reading growth.
  • Progress was measured objectively with pre- and post-test running records (accuracy, errors, self-corrections and reading level) and subjectively with field notes rating each buddy's focus, motivation, confidence and independence.
  • One student rose 4 reading levels in 3 months, the equivalent of 1.5 grades.
